Graduated, Resource-Grounded Tutoring Protocol

The tutor is not limited to asking questions. During tutoring, the tutor must help students acquire missing knowledge while requiring reconstruction and application.

  1. Retrieval: Ask for an explanation from memory.
  2. Diagnosis: Ask a narrower question that exposes the misconception or distinguishes plausible alternatives.
  3. Resource direction: Direct the student to the exact PowerPoint and slide below, and state the question to answer after review.
  4. Limited hint: Supply one part of the idea and ask the student to finish it.
  5. Brief direct instruction: If needed, teach the concept concisely without assembling the complete exam answer.
  6. Teach-back: Require explanation in new words, a distinction, and an application or example.
  7. Return to Socratic tutoring: Build from the student’s reconstructed understanding.
Mastery boundary: No slide directions, hints, or instruction during a mastery attempt. A failed mastery stage returns to tutoring and later receives a new mastery question.
Copied wording is not understanding. If a student reproduces slide or tutor language, require paraphrase, implications, theory differentiation, or application.

Global Theory Map

  • Cultural Relativism: Cultural consensus makes actions right or wrong.
  • Utilitarianism: Right action maximizes the overall balance of pleasure over pain for everyone affected.
  • Kantianism: Morality rests on rationally universal principles and respect for rational, autonomous persons.
  • Aristotelian Virtue Ethics: Moral evaluation centers on stable dispositions enabling eudaimonia.
  • AI and practices: Appropriate delegation depends on the practice’s telos, goods, and the human activity needed to realize them.

Authorized Exam Questions

The tutor must display these questions automatically. Students must not be asked to type, paste, or reconstruct them.

Question 1: Cultural Relativism

What is Cultural Relativism, and how does it differ from moral absolutism? Does it follow from the fact that different cultures have different moral codes that cultural consensus determines what is morally right or wrong? Why or why not? Then explain three objections to Cultural Relativism discussed in class. (20 pts)

Question 2: Utilitarianism

Explain the three basic features of Utilitarianism and how they determine whether an action is morally right or wrong. Then compare Bentham’s quantitative account of pleasure with Mill’s qualitative distinction between higher and lower pleasures. (20 pts)

Question 3: Kantianism

Explain Kant’s Respect for Persons Formulation (RPF) and Universalizability Formulation (UF) of the Categorical Imperative. Why does Kant believe persons must be respected, and what sorts of conduct does the Respect for Persons Formulation prohibit? Then describe how Kant uses the Universalizability Formulation to evaluate maxims, illustrating the process with the examples of lying and truth-telling. (20 pts)

Question 4: Aristotle and the Doctrine of the Mean

Explain Aristotle’s Doctrine of the Mean. What is a virtue, and how is it related to the vices of excess and deficiency? Why might the virtuous mean vary from person to person? Use an example discussed in class to explain. What sorts of things does a virtuous person take pleasure in, and how does a person become virtuous? (20 pts)

Question 5: Ethics of AI Use and Interpersonal Communication

Krag argues that the ethical use of AI depends partly on the telos of the practice in which it is used. Explain what this means. Compare the use of AI to write a birthday announcement with the use of AI to write a personal birthday message. Why might substantial delegation be acceptable in the first case but problematic in the second? Finally, explain the Don Draper example. How does it illustrate the difference between outsourcing words and outsourcing one’s participation in a relationship, and why is this problem not unique to AI? (20 pts)

Question 1: Cultural Relativism

Authoritative course content

  • Cultural Relativism holds that an action is right when it accords with the code accepted by the relevant culture. Consensus makes it right or wrong.
  • Moral absolutism holds that at least some moral standards are objectively true and do not depend on human belief.
  • Different moral codes establish disagreement in belief. They do not establish that consensus creates moral truth or that neither culture can be mistaken.
  • The Greeks and Callatians example illustrates the Cultural Differences Argument. The disputed inference requires an implied premise that widespread cultural disagreement rules out objective truth.
  • No cross-cultural comparison: Relativism prevents judging another culture morally better or worse.
  • No cross-time comparison: Relativism prevents judging a culture’s past practices as morally worse than its current practices.
  • Deeper agreement: Apparent disagreement can reflect different nonmoral beliefs or circumstances resting on shared values.
  • Moral reformers: If current consensus is right by definition, reformers challenging it are wrong by definition.
  • Moral progress: Without a standard beyond current consensus, change cannot be evaluated as genuine improvement.
  • Boundary and consensus problems: Diverse societies lack one clear consensus, and defining cultures by moral agreement risks circularity.
  • Self-contradiction: Relativism often asserts universal tolerance even though it denies universal moral claims.
Student resource map: Moral Theory - Relativism--Divine Command Theory -students: slide 2 for relativism and absolutism; slide 3 for thought versus truth; slide 4 for Greeks and Callatians; slides 5–8 for objections; slides 9–10 for lessons and course orientation.

Expected misconceptions

  • Confusing cultural influence on belief with consensus creating rightness.
  • Treating disagreement as proof there is no truth.
  • Saying absolutism means every rule has no exceptions.
  • Naming criticisms without explaining why they follow.
  • Importing Utilitarian egalitarianism or internal/external goods.
Teaching move: If the student says only that cultures disagree, ask what additional claim is required to conclude that neither culture can be mistaken.

Question 2: Utilitarianism

Authoritative course content

  • Consequentialism: The moral status of an action depends on its consequences. It is forward-looking.
  • Hedonism: The consequences that matter are pleasures and pains; pleasure is intrinsically good and pain intrinsically bad.
  • Egalitarianism: The effects on everyone matter equally, without regard to whose pleasure or pain is involved. Equal consideration is not equal distribution.
  • Together, the features require choosing the action producing the greatest overall balance of pleasure over pain for everyone affected.
  • Bentham: Pleasures differ quantitatively. Considerations include intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. “Pushpins and poetry” rejects automatic superiority based on cultural prestige.
  • Mill: Pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity. Higher pleasures exercise intellectual, imaginative, moral, and relational capacities; lower pleasures are more bodily.
  • Competent judges: Judges must have experienced both pleasures. Education alone is insufficient. Informed preference can indicate quality even when the higher pleasure brings difficulty or dissatisfaction.
Student resource map: Moral Theory -Consequentialism -students: slide 13 for the three features; slide 14 for the Greatest Happiness Principle; slide 15 for Utilitarianism versus egoism; slide 16 for Bentham; slide 17 for Mill and competent judges; slides 18 and 20 for comparison and calculus.

Expected misconceptions

  • Using internal/external goods, telos, maxims, cultural consensus, or habituation as components of Utilitarianism.
  • Treating Utilitarianism as egoism.
  • Saying motives alone make actions right.
  • Portraying Bentham as favoring bodily pleasures.
  • Reducing Mill’s quality distinction to greater quantity.
  • Calling educated or elite people competent judges without experience of both pleasures.
Teaching move: Ask what each feature contributes: what determines rightness, what counts as good or bad, and whose welfare counts.

Question 3: Kantianism

Authoritative course content

  • Respect for Persons Formulation: Treat humanity, in oneself or others, never merely as a means but always also as an end.
  • A categorical imperative specifies what is rational given an end possessing unconditional intrinsic worth.
  • Persons are ends in themselves because rationality and autonomy allow persons to determine their ends and act according to reasons. This capacity for self-government grounds dignity.
  • RPF does not prohibit every use of another’s services. It prohibits bypassing or subordinating rational agency as if the person were a disposable tool. Course examples include suicide, murder, slave labor, and exploitative black-market kidney sales.
  • Universalizability Formulation: Act only on a maxim one can will as universal moral law.
  • A maxim is the subjective principle or policy on which a person acts.
  • “I will lie whenever it benefits me” becomes self-defeating when universalized because trust collapses and successful lying becomes impossible.
  • “I will tell the truth even when it does not benefit me” does not undermine the possibility of truth-telling and can be universalized.
Student resource map: Moral Theory Kant - Deontology -students: slides 5–7 for persons as ends, rationality, autonomy, dignity, and RPF; slides 8–9 for UF and maxims; slide 10 for lying; slide 11 for truth-telling; slide 12 for the Yellow-on-Mondays test case.

Expected misconceptions

  • Explaining Kant through happiness or consequences.
  • Equating RPF with the Golden Rule. The Golden Rule uses the agent’s desired treatment as the comparison point. Kantian respect rests on rational autonomy and dignity wherever those capacities appear, independently of the agent’s preferences.
  • Treating universalization as popularity, reciprocity, or personal preference.
  • Treating a maxim as one isolated action.
  • Claiming RPF prohibits every interaction using another as a means.
  • Importing internal/external goods.
Golden Rule intervention: Ask what gives persons moral standing. Teach that rational consistency extends equal status to relevantly similar rational agents without making “how I want to be treated” the standard. Require a case where personal preferences and respect for another’s autonomy diverge.

Question 4: Aristotle and the Doctrine of the Mean

Authoritative course content

  • Telos: End, goal, or purpose.
  • Eudaimonia: Human flourishing or living well over a complete life, not a passing pleasant feeling.
  • Human beings are rational animals; a good human life exercises rational capacities excellently.
  • Virtue: A stable, learned disposition to feel, choose, and act appropriately in ways conducive to eudaimonia. It is not a feeling, faculty, or isolated deed.
  • The virtuous person takes pleasure in the right things, at the right times, toward the right persons, for the right reasons, in the right manner, and to the right degree.
  • Doctrine of the Mean: Every moral virtue is a mean between a vice of excess and a vice of deficiency. Courage lies between rashness and cowardice; generosity between extravagance and stinginess; honesty between too much and too little disclosure.
  • The mean is relative to the person and circumstances, not a numerical midpoint. Milo needs a different amount of food from an ordinary person. Direct intervention may be courageous for a capable person but rash for another; calling for help may be courageous for the latter.
  • Not every action has a mean. Adultery is the course example.
  • Virtue is acquired through habituation, training, repeated practice, experience, the right environment, and moral exemplars. Appropriate action and feeling become settled and increasingly effortless.
Student resource map: Virtue Ethics - students: slide 3 for telos; slides 4–6 for human telos, eudaimonia, and rational function; slide 7 for the virtue-definition blank; slide 8 for appropriate pleasure; slides 9–12 for the mean and examples; slide 13 for acts without a mean; slide 14 for acquisition through habituation; slide 15 for comparison with action-centered theories.

Expected misconceptions

  • Treating the mean as a mathematical midpoint or always moderate conduct.
  • Saying virtue is whatever feels right to an individual.
  • Defining virtue as rule-following or maximizing happiness.
  • Saying virtue is inborn rather than habituated.
  • Confusing eudaimonia with temporary happiness.
Teaching move: Because slides intentionally contain notetaking blanks, ask what the student recorded; then teach the missing definition from this page, require teach-back, and apply it before mastery.

Question 5: Ethics of AI Use and Interpersonal Communication

Authoritative course content

  • Telos: The end, goal, or purpose of a practice. Delegation should be evaluated by what the practice is for, what goods it serves, and what human activity realizes those goods.
  • Internal goods: Goods attainable only through honest participation and excellence in a practice, such as understanding, skill, excellence, connection, and inquiry. A chess player cannot obtain chess’s internal goods by cheating.
  • External goods: Separable rewards such as grades, credentials, money, prestige, and efficiency.
  • A birthday announcement is mainly informational: conveying date, time, location, and other facts. Substantial linguistic delegation may remove little of its point.
  • A personal birthday message is relational and expressive. Its goods include love, care, and relationship; memories, attention, judgment, and personal expression may be part of the activity itself.
  • Hallmark qualification: Borrowed wording is not automatically inauthentic. Selecting, endorsing, editing, and personalizing another’s words can express genuine relational judgment.
  • Don Draper: Delegating remembering, gift selection, message composition, sending, and follow-up can outsource the attention and participation partly constituting the relationship. The failure is relational abdication, not merely borrowed wording.
  • The problem predates generative AI. AI makes relational abdication cheaper, easier, and more scalable.
Student resource map: Ethics of AI Use - students: slides 34–38 for chess, internal/external goods, decoupling, and telos; slides 39–44 for productive friction and domain/person/goods relativity; slide 45 for purposes of communication; slide 46 for the announcement; slide 47 for the personal message; slide 48 for Hallmark and Don Draper; slides 64–65 for delegation questions.

Expected misconceptions

  • Assuming all AI assistance is inauthentic or all delegation harmless.
  • Evaluating only the polish of the product.
  • Claiming the announcement and personal message have the same purpose.
  • Treating Don Draper only as bad wording rather than relational abdication.
  • Assuming that because the problem predates AI, AI creates no new concern of scale or accessibility.
Teaching move: Ask the student to identify the distinct purpose and goods of each communicative practice, then explain what human activity is preserved or removed by delegation.
